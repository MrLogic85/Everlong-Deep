# Pirate Game: Faction Design

Oct 27, 2026 · Fredrik

## What factions are

Factions are the driving forces behind the world. A faction can control a port, a ship or a fleet.

Factions exist on a continuum, from a single ship to an empire of ports and fleets. A faction is a group of people loyal to something, at whatever scale.

Factions also drive why rumors travel: their members move between ports on faction business. See the rumor system doc.

## Focus areas and allegiances

Each faction has a focus area. It might lean toward trading, piracy, or government of one or more ports. A faction can do it all, but there are consequences.

Most of those consequences lie in factions' allegiances toward one another. Greed and jealousy built into the NPCs keep all the factions from simply aligning.

## Birth, splits and death

Factions can split, die and be born. This is driven by NPC wants, not by separate faction logic.

Example: a merchant vessel defects, turns pirate and steals its cargo. A new one-ship faction is born, with a former employer who wants the cargo back, and a rumor about all of it.

Fracture needs a counterweight. If defecting is easy and nothing holds people together, the world dissolves into countless one-ship factions. Reasons to join and stay, such as protection, steady pay, safe ports or a shared enemy, balance the wants that split groups apart.

## Keeping any faction from taking over

The hard problem is avoiding one big faction controlling all ports and wiping out all competition. Static factions with fixed ports and a maximum number of vessels would solve it, but that goes against a living world.

The goal is not to stop factions from growing, but to make large size unstable, so the world goes through cycles of rise and fall.

| Brake | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| Distance weakens control | Information travels by ship, so distant ports report and receive orders late, and remote governors start acting on their own |
| Big factions break from within | More members means more ambitious captains and governors who want more power, so large factions are the most likely to split or rebel |
| Jealousy points at the leader | Greed and jealousy push rivals to unite against whoever is strongest |
| A monopoly starves itself | With all competition gone, routes become safe, and safe routes don't pay |

The headless simulation can test this: run it for decades and check that the number of factions doesn't converge to one.

## The player and factions

Whether the player can grow into a full faction is undecided. It's doable, but the game should be about the ship you own, not a macro strategy game.

The direction being considered: **you own it, they run it.** Faction control comes from having the right people on your side, such as someone good at governing a port or a merchant good at trading.

- A crew member who wants to set up shop somewhere becomes how your faction grows. You own the shop, but you don't personally control it.
- You're just one person and can't do everything, so you put people you trust in charge of different things. It's the same setup as on the ship: you set rules for who controls what and give orders, which people might follow.
- Coming back, you might find the shop has grown, closed because its keeper felt like leaving, or closed due to financial problems, perhaps leaving you in debt to the port's faction.
- In a full sandbox you could take over a shop yourself, order crew to run it, or have crew sail the ship without you, leaving you stranded.

This adds a lot of complexity that isn't good to start with, but is worth keeping in mind.

## Open questions

- Should the player be able to grow into a full faction?
- What does owning something give the player: money, a safe harbor, reliable information, influence?
- How do factions act on their own, such as waging war, blockading ports or taking control of a town?
- What holds a faction together strongly enough to balance the wants that split it?
