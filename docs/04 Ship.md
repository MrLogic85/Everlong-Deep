# Pirate Game: Ship Design

Oct 26, 2026 · Fredrik

## What a ship is

A ship is four things at once, and they pull on each other.

| Role | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Home | The space where the crew colony lives |
| Machine | Something that sails and fights, with performance that depends on its crew |
| Identity | A name and reputation that outlive any captain |
| Asset | Something you earn, risk and can lose |

The same hull handles differently with a skilled crew than with landlubbers. A ship's performance is never just its stats.

## Getting a ship

How you get a ship decides what history comes with it.

| Way | What it involves | What comes with it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Steal in harbor | Undecided: a planned heist, an opportunistic grab, or both | The previous owner's reputation, and a ship that will be recognized |
| Capture at sea | Combat and boarding | The same: the ship's legacy and a hunting former owner |
| Commission | Money and time at a shipyard | A clean slate, but the shipyard knows who ordered it |

The choice of how to acquire a ship is also a reputation choice.

## Configuration

Configuring a ship means arranging a physical layout, limited by the ship's design. Where things are decides how long things take.

- **Placed on the layout:** cannons (how many, where, what kind), ammunition (how much, where, what kind), gun racks (how many, where, what kind), storage (where, how much), and quarters (where, how many crew, what quality).
- **Chosen as upgrades:** hull upgrades, and sails (shape, material, quality).

Distance costs time. Ammunition far from the cannons means slower reloading. Gun racks far from where boarders come over the side means the crew arm too late. Two layouts of the same ship fight differently, and the player can see why.

Space is a trade-off. Room spent on comfortable quarters isn't spent on cargo or guns, and quarter quality feeds crew mood. A packed hold pays off in profit and costs in crew mood on a long voyage.

Every option should create a choice the player feels. Options that interact with space and crew come first; others are added once it's clear what new decision each creates.

## Crewing

A ship has stations, such as sails, helm, guns and boarding defense. Each needs a number of hands to work well. Fewer hands degrade a station instead of switching it off.

- Too few on the rigging means slower turns and less sail, not a ship that can't move.
- Guns drive crew size, because each cannon needs its own gun crew.

The crew requirement comes from the configuration. Place many cannons and you've created the need for many gunners. A big ship stripped of guns can be sailed by fewer people, trading firepower for something you can staff.

Crew priorities are part of the ship's rules: man the cannons, load them, or arm themselves before boarding or repelling boarders.

## Sailing and fighting

The captain is a crew member who can take any station. Whatever you don't do yourself, someone else does, according to your rules.

| Function | Do it yourself | Command it | Delegate it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Steering | Man the rudder | | Give the helm to a crew member |
| Guns | | Call out volleys, or fire at will | Give gun command to a crew member |
| Fighting | Pick up a gun yourself | | Let the crew do the rest |

**Decisions are the player's, execution is the character's.** Player skill covers things like timing shots and positioning. Character stats cover things like reload speed and accuracy. The exact split is worked out station by station later.

Because any station can be delegated, the crew must handle every one competently. A mediocre crew member should be worse than the player and an excellent one better, so hiring well frees you to do what you enjoy.

**One fighting system everywhere.** Fights on your own deck, boarding another ship, and violence in port all use the same systems. So combat has to be designed for fighting in a space in general, with ship-specific parts like guns and sails on top. See the port doc.

## Identity and fame

Every ship has a reputation built into the world from the start, and it passes to whoever takes the ship over.

- **Fame scales with notability.** The governor's flagship is legendary; most sloops are nobody.
- **Anonymity is a resource.** Small, unknown ships let a player disappear.
- **Identity can be shed, at a risk.** A pirate port can fence a stolen ship or repaint it to get rid of the old identity, though it may still be recognized.

## Why not steal the biggest ship

Stealing a big ship early is possible, but usually ruinous. The brakes come from existing systems, not artificial gates.

| Brake | Why it bites |
| --- | --- |
| You can't crew it | Too few hands degrade every station, and hiring in a hurry means the worst crew composition |
| You can't afford it | Every extra crew member costs pay and provisions on every voyage |
| It's famous | Big ships carry big reputations, their owners hunt them, and the theft itself becomes a story |

Guards make a theft harder, but they're an obstacle, not a reason to avoid it.

The costs should be visible before the player commits, for example through crew saying they can't sail her with so few hands. A cost discovered days later at sea feels like a trap.

**Example heist:** sell your current ship, hire a crew with the money, steal a big ship, then take it to a pirate port to sell or repaint. Kill the whole crew afterwards to stop gossip about the heist, and a new rumor appears anyway: people remember the forty sailors you hired, and none of them came home. The captain whose crews never return will struggle to hire again.

## Open questions

- Is stealing a ship in harbor a planned heist, an opportunistic grab, or both?
- Which configuration options create real choices, and which are just more or less of the same thing for more money?
- How is the split between player skill and character stats set for each station?
- How much does sailing itself simulate, such as wind and weather?
- How does a fleet of several ships work?
